Most introductions to Python quietly assume you already think in equations. If you come from the wet-lab bench, with plate readers, spreadsheets, and experimental protocols rather than a mathematics or statistics background, the first hurdle is not the syntax. It is the idea that a program is simply a list of instructions for a worker who follows them exactly.

Having made the transition from bench experimentalist to computational biology myself, these two short videos are my attempt to remove that hurdle, one small step at a time.

The idea behind the videos

The goal is to help complete beginners understand how to work with Python without any quantitative background. The first video starts from a situation most biochemists and cell biologists know well: a plate reader returns 96 numbers across dozens of plates, and someone has to count, add, and check them without losing track of a row. It then walks through core built-in functions (len(), sum(), max(), abs(), round(), sorted(), any(), all()) framed as a conversation between a Client and a Worker. The second video explains the small building blocks you need to read and write Python code: comparison operators, and how a function call is put together.

Video 1: Why a script beats copy and paste & built-in functions (8 min)

This video introduces Python as a language for giving instructions, base Python as a toolkit of ready-made functions (counting, totals, tidying, sorting, and checking conditions), and specialist libraries that you bring in when the built-in tools are not enough.

Video 2: Operators and function structure (5 min)

This video covers the assignment and comparison operators (=, ==, !=, <, >, <=, >=), and then the anatomy of a function call: the name, the round brackets, and the arguments inside them.

How the videos were made

Rather than recording a screen by hand, the whole series is generated deterministically from code:

  • Manim, a Python animation library, draws the operator and function-structure scenes.
  • React.js, HTML, CSS, and JavaScript, rendered frame by frame in a headless Chromium browser, make the opening plate-reader spreadsheet and closing scenes.
  • Kokoro, an offline text-to-speech model, provides the English narration.
  • ffmpeg mixes the narration into the picture and joins the scenes.
  • Claude, an AI coding assistant, wrote the scripts and the animation code, guided by reusable skill instructions for screencast production (part of the agentic workflows I explored in my previous post).

Every scene is generated from version-controlled text. That makes it easy to correct a sentence or tweak an example and rebuild only the part that changed.

Who this is for

If you know what a 96-well plate and a spreadsheet are, you can follow these videos. If something is unclear, the scenes are short enough to watch again, and the next step is simply to open a Python console and try the same lines yourself.
